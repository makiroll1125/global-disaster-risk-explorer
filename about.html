Behind the patterns.

A university data visualization project by Tobias Garcia and Ha Nguyen. We explore where major natural disasters are recorded, how their reported impacts differ, and how those patterns change over time.

01 / THE EVIDENCE

Dataset

We use the supplied EM-DAT public natural-disaster export from CRED / UCLouvain, dated 15 September 2026. The original Excel workbook is retained in data/raw/; the dashboard loads the cleaned local JSON, with no live data service.

10,896country-disaster records
220countries and territories
14natural disaster types
2000–2026start years; 2026 is partial

Raw dataset

The XLSX contains 10,896 rows covering start years 2000–2026. Attributes include disaster identifiers, type/subtype, country, ISO code, region, start year/month/day, deaths, affected population and nominal/adjusted damage.

A row describes a disaster's impact in one country. The same event can appear in several countries: these rows correspond to 9,001 distinct event identifiers, not 10,896 unique worldwide events.

Processed dataset

All 10,896 rows fall within the selected scope and remain in the processed data. The five source regions are Africa, Americas, Asia, Europe and Oceania. We retain the source country and type names; labels may be shortened for display.

disasters.json contains individual records and audit metadata; disasters.csv provides the same records for tabular analysis.

The browser filters individual records to build map totals, lines and rankings. It stacks annual type counts for the streamgraph and constructs a World → Region → Country → Disaster Type hierarchy for the treemap. This keeps all views tied to the same counting method.

Data cleaning and preprocessing

  1. Inspect and scope. Locate the EM-DAT header, trim text, validate required fields and restrict start years to 2000–2026 and all natural hazards. This supplied export needed no scope exclusions.
  2. Validate records and geography. Remove exact duplicates and reject conflicting duplicate identifiers. This export had no duplicates. Uppercase and validate ISO codes, derive event IDs from country-record IDs, and join numeric ISO codes to Natural Earth / World Atlas polygons. Source country/type names are not reclassified.
  3. Preserve unknown impacts. Parse numerical fields; invalid, negative or nonfinite impacts become null. A blank is never converted to zero. Sum only reported values and carry reporting counts alongside each aggregate.
  4. Normalize units and dates. Convert source damage values from thousands of US dollars to dollars; preserve the source's 2025 adjusted-price basis. Validate start months as integers 1–12 and create nullable year-month values. Count a multi-month disaster once in its recorded start month.
  5. Export and audit. Write cleaned JSON/CSV, grouped tables, impact distributions, largest-impact records and a quality report with the original file's SHA-256 checksum.

Verified against prepare_data.py, the supplied workbook, quality-report.json and the processed records. Reproduction commands and full definitions are in Data methods and the preprocessing guide.

What the numbers cannot tell us

  • Partial reporting: 2026 is incomplete; its adjusted damage is unavailable. It should not be compared with complete years as if it were fully observed.
  • Monthly coverage: 10,823 rows (99.33%) have valid start months; 73 do not. One November 2026 start month lies after the export date. Monthly analysis uses 10,822 records, excluding that row and missing months; annual data retains them. October–December 2026 are unavailable, and September is partial.
  • Seasonal denominator: month counts are divided by the number of selected years excluding all of 2026. For 2000–2004, each month uses five years. Missing dates may understate any month, and complete calendar years do not guarantee complete reporting.
  • Impact coverage: 3,070 rows lack deaths, 2,076 lack affected population, and 7,590 lack adjusted damage. Blank means unknown/unreported, not zero; totals are reported sums, not estimates of missing impacts.
  • Geography: 279 records lack a matching map polygon; they stay in totals and other charts. Counts are not population-adjusted. Major recorded disasters do not represent every hazard occurrence; affected totals can count the same person across events.

02 / WORKING WITH REAL RECORDS

Visualizations

Six implemented views answer complementary questions. The three previews below are genuine captures of the working dashboard using the full supplied dataset, all natural hazards and the record-count measure (2000–2026). Open each chart to use its interactive controls.

Disaster-type streamgraph

Annual record counts by type → colored layer thickness. The full 2000–2026 context remains visible; the historical interval is outlined. How does the mix of recorded disasters change? Vertical position is a flowing baseline, not a total axis.

Seasonal patterns

Start-month counts divided by selected complete years → January–December bar heights. Which calendar months have higher average recorded frequency for a selected hazard or place?

Geographical treemap

Filtered country/type record counts → rectangle area, with region hues and stable country shades. How are records distributed geographically, and which types make up each country's total?

Two independent clocks, one set of category filters

The map's period and playback year affect only the map. The historical range drives the line, summaries, rankings, seasonal averages and treemap, and highlights an interval in the full-history streamgraph. Changing historical aggregation affects only the line chart. Impact measure, disaster type, region and country remain shared filters.

Treemap breadcrumbs change only its local location. Reset to World preserves the historical range, measure and all global filters: “World” means the overview within those filters. Clear a global geography filter in the Explorer when you want every region again.

04 / EVALUATING THE EXPERIENCE

Evaluation Plan

Planned study; no participant results are claimed. We will recruit approximately 5–10 university students unfamiliar with the application. Each participant will use the working dashboard and the actual supplied dataset in a short, moderated session.

What we will evaluate

Can people identify high-count countries, compare disaster types and impacts, recognize historical changes, use the two time controls independently, and navigate World → Region → Country and back? We will also assess color/legend interpretation, the distinction between frequency, human impact and economic damage, readability, responsiveness and navigation clarity.

How we will evaluate it

Start each task from a specified filter state. Record independent success before offering help, ask participants to think aloud, and verify their answers against the processed records. Include desktop and a smaller-screen task.

  1. With Flood selected and no geography filter, find the country with the most records in 2000–2004. Use the ranking and explain what one record represents.
  2. Set map playback to 2010–2015 while retaining a 2000–2004 historical range. Start playback and demonstrate that the historical selection stays unchanged.
  3. Explore the historical record count for 2000–2004, switch to Monthly, and explain why monthly totals may differ from annual totals.
  4. Open Asia and then China in the treemap. Inspect its disaster-type composition; use Back and then Reset to World without changing global filters.
  5. Compare Flood and Storm in the streamgraph in 2000 and 2020 using tooltips. Explain the difference between layer thickness, vertical position and impact severity.
  6. Switch from record counts to deaths or adjusted damage. Interpret the units and a missing-value indicator, then locate the dataset limitations.

Before sessions, the team will prepare an answer sheet from disasters.json for those exact filters, including ties and missing dates. Task wording may be piloted with one volunteer before the main study.

Feedback and evidence we will collect

Task observations

  • Completion rate and time per task.
  • Incorrect selections, wrong-chart time changes, navigation errors and assistance requests.
  • Accuracy of answers and explanations of colors, areas, units and missing values.

Participant feedback

  • Short 1–5 ratings for ease of filtering, navigation, readability and confidence.
  • Which controls or encodings were confusing, and why.
  • Open comments and suggestions for improvement.

We will summarize success rates, median completion times and recurring problems, then prioritize changes to labels, controls, visual encodings and navigation before final submission. Automated real-data and browser checks already support correctness testing; they do not replace this planned user study.
